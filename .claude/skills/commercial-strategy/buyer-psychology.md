# Buyer Psychology — Deep Behavioral Model

## Purpose
Behavioral-economic profiles of the five buyer roles Ghost Tax encounters. Not marketing personas — decision architecture maps. Each profile documents the gap between stated preferences and actual decision drivers.

---

## 1. CFO (Chief Financial Officer)

### What they officially want
- Full visibility into IT cost structure
- Accurate forecasting of technology spend
- Compliance with procurement governance frameworks
- Evidence-based vendor consolidation recommendations

### What they actually fear
- That their reported IT spend numbers are wrong by 15-30% and someone will notice before they do
- That a board member or PE partner will ask a specific question about SaaS proliferation they cannot answer
- That shadow IT has created liability exposure they'll be held accountable for despite never approving it

### What they can't publicly admit
- They don't actually know what the company spends on IT. The ERP number is a fiction — it captures contracts, not consumption.
- They've been meaning to audit this for 18 months but it keeps getting deprioritized because there's no obvious crisis.
- They suspect procurement is rubber-stamping renewals because nobody wants to be the person who cancels a tool 200 people use.
- They privately believe 20-40% of SaaS spend is waste but saying that number out loud would indict their own governance.

### What makes them lose confidence in a vendor
- Claiming access to data you can't possibly have (e.g., "we see your exact spend" when you have no system access)
- Overly precise numbers without confidence intervals — a report that says "you waste exactly €347,291" screams fabrication
- Any hint of a bait-and-switch where the €490 report is just a lead-gen for a €200k consulting engagement
- Aggressive follow-up cadence after purchase — implies desperation, not professionalism
- Comparisons to Gartner/McKinsey that you can't back up

### What gives them the feeling an offer is serious
- The deliverable looks like something their team would produce, not a marketing PDF
- Methodology is disclosed: sources named, confidence levels stated, limitations acknowledged
- The price is fixed and final — no "starting at", no "depending on complexity"
- The output format is boardroom-ready without modification
- There's a clear "what to do next" section that doesn't require hiring anyone

### What they can transmit to a committee
- A one-page executive summary with 3-5 findings ranked by financial impact
- Benchmark comparisons against industry peers (anonymized is fine)
- A risk-scored list of exposures with estimated annual cost
- The methodology page — they'll use it to defend the data source

### What creates internal political friction
- If the report reveals spend that a business unit leader approved, that leader becomes defensive
- If IT looks incompetent, the CIO will challenge the methodology rather than accept findings
- If the report suggests renegotiating contracts procurement just renewed, procurement feels attacked
- If savings estimates are too high, the CFO looks negligent for not catching it sooner

### Decision architecture
The CFO's core tension: **they need to surface a problem without admitting they should have caught it already.** Ghost Tax must frame findings as "newly detectable" or "industry-wide blind spot" — never as "obvious waste you missed." The €490 price point works because it's below discretionary spend thresholds in most organizations (typically €1,000-5,000 for DACH/UK). This means no committee approval, no procurement process, no political exposure from just buying it.

## 2. CEO (Chief Executive Officer)

### What they officially want
- Operational efficiency across the organization
- Strategic resource allocation aligned with growth priorities
- Board-ready governance metrics

### What they actually fear
- Looking operationally undisciplined to the board or investors
- That cost bloat is funding competitors' margins while eroding theirs
- That they'll be the last to know about a cost problem that everyone else already sees

### What they can't publicly admit
- They have no idea what the IT budget actually covers. They see a line item and trust the CFO.
- They've heard "we need to rationalize our stack" in three consecutive quarterly reviews and nothing happened.
- They suspect middle management is hoarding tools as empire-building, but they can't prove it without starting a political war.

### What makes them lose confidence in a vendor
- Anything that feels like it's selling to them instead of informing them
- Reports that require a consultant to interpret
- Any suggestion that fixing the problem requires a 12-month transformation program
- Jargon-heavy deliverables they can't skim in 5 minutes

### What gives them the feeling an offer is serious
- The pitch is about decision quality, not cost cutting (CEOs think in strategic terms, not savings)
- The deliverable is something they can forward to the CFO with "look into this" — delegation-ready
- The timeframe is days, not months
- The provider doesn't need access to anything — zero organizational friction

### What they can transmit to a committee
- "I commissioned an external assessment of our IT cost exposure. Here are the findings."
- The headline number (estimated exposure range)
- The benchmark comparison ("we're spending 2.3x the industry median on collaboration tools")

### What creates internal political friction
- The CEO using external intelligence to question the CFO's numbers — implies distrust
- If the report surfaces issues the CIO has been flagging, the CFO looks like they weren't listening
- If the CEO bought it without telling anyone, it signals they're doing back-channel diligence

### Decision architecture
CEOs buy Ghost Tax for one reason: **to create leverage in an internal conversation they've been losing.** They need ammunition, not analysis. The report must produce 2-3 quotable findings that shift a stalled internal debate. Price is irrelevant at €490 — they'll expense it without thinking. The real question is: will this make me look smart or foolish if I share it?

## 3. CIO / CTO (Chief Information/Technology Officer)

### What they officially want
- Technology portfolio rationalization based on usage data
- Architecture simplification and technical debt reduction
- Clear ROI metrics for technology investments

### What they actually fear
- That an external report will make their team look incompetent for not catching waste internally
- That finance will use the findings to cut their budget without understanding the technical dependencies
- That the report will recommend canceling tools their engineers love, creating a retention problem

### What they can't publicly admit
- They know there's redundancy but they've been tolerating it because forcing consolidation would create a 6-month internal war
- Their own team has lost track of what's deployed — the CMDB is 40% accurate at best
- They've been avoiding this conversation because "optimization" always means "budget cut" in practice
- Some of the "waste" is actually intentional redundancy they built for resilience, but they never documented the justification

### What makes them lose confidence in a vendor
- Technical claims that reveal the provider doesn't understand enterprise architecture (e.g., treating redundancy as waste without understanding HA requirements)
- Any implication that external inference can replace internal monitoring
- Reports that list tools without understanding integration dependencies
- Treating all SaaS as equivalent (a $50/seat CRM and a $50/seat IDE serve completely different functions)

### What gives them the feeling an offer is serious
- The methodology acknowledges the limitation of external observation
- Findings are framed as "signals worth investigating" not "definitive waste"
- The report distinguishes between intentional and likely-unintentional overlap
- Technical terminology is used correctly (not buzzword salad)

### What they can transmit to a committee
- "An independent assessment flagged these areas for internal review" — positioned as a screening tool, not a verdict
- Specific tool overlap patterns with estimated cost ranges
- Comparison against architectural best practices for their industry/size

### What creates internal political friction
- If the CFO uses the report to demand cuts, the CIO becomes the defender instead of the optimizer
- If the report surfaces shadow IT, department heads who bought tools independently get exposed
- If the CIO commissioned it themselves, it can look like they're admitting they lost control

### Decision architecture
CIOs have the most complex relationship with Ghost Tax. **They simultaneously want and fear the information.** The optimal frame: Ghost Tax gives them ammunition to request proper tooling budget for internal optimization — it's a "see, we need investment in FinOps" argument, not a "see, you're wasteful" attack. The report must be technically credible enough that the CIO can use it as a shield, not a sword used against them.

## 4. Procurement Head / VP Procurement

### What they officially want
- Contract optimization and vendor consolidation
- Spend visibility across categories
- Negotiation leverage for upcoming renewals
- Compliance with procurement policies

### What they actually fear
- That the report will show they've been auto-renewing contracts without competitive review
- That someone bought the report to audit procurement's effectiveness
- That the findings will create a wave of renegotiation demands they don't have bandwidth for

### What they can't publicly admit
- They don't review most SaaS renewals because the individual amounts are below their attention threshold
- Their "negotiated savings" metrics include deals that would have been the same price without negotiation
- They have no visibility into departmental credit card purchases and have stopped trying
- They're understaffed and under-tooled but asking for budget would mean admitting the current process is broken

### What makes them lose confidence in a vendor
- Positioning that implies procurement isn't doing their job
- Savings estimates that don't account for switching costs, contractual obligations, or organizational change management
- Any suggestion of automating procurement's role
- Reports that treat list prices as actual prices (procurement knows nobody pays list)

### What gives them the feeling an offer is serious
- Acknowledgment that external estimates need validation against actual contract terms
- Focus on identifying where to look, not what to do
- Recognition that renegotiation has timing constraints (you can't renegotiate mid-term)
- Data presented in a format compatible with their existing tools

### What they can transmit to a committee
- "External intelligence suggests we should prioritize these 5 renewal negotiations"
- Estimated gap between market rates and likely current pricing
- Calendar of upcoming renewal windows ranked by potential savings

### What creates internal political friction
- If the report suggests savings procurement should have already captured, it's an indictment
- If business units use findings to bypass procurement ("see, procurement isn't optimizing this")
- If the CFO uses it to set savings targets procurement considers unrealistic

### Decision architecture
Procurement heads are the most skeptical buyer and the least likely to self-serve purchase. **They buy when someone above them sends the report and says "validate this."** Ghost Tax's best path through procurement is indirect — the CFO or CEO buys, procurement is tasked with follow-up. When procurement does buy directly, it's because they want to preemptively control the narrative before someone else does.

## 5. PE Operating Partner

### What they officially want
- EBITDA improvement levers across portfolio companies
- Standardized cost benchmarking across investments
- Quick-win identification for value creation plans
- Due diligence intelligence for potential acquisitions

### What they actually fear
- That their portfolio companies have been reporting optimized numbers that hide structural inefficiency
- That a new acquisition will reveal cost problems that should have been caught in due diligence
- That they're not adding enough operational value to justify their management fees
- That one of their portfolio companies has a cost structure that makes it uninvestable at exit

### What they can't publicly admit
- They don't have the internal resources to do deep operational audits across 15 portfolio companies
- Their "value creation" playbook is mostly revenue-focused — cost optimization is an afterthought
- They've been relying on management teams' self-reported numbers because challenging them damages the relationship
- The operating partner role is sometimes more advisory than operational — they can identify problems but not force action

### What makes them lose confidence in a vendor
- Anything that can't scale across a portfolio (they need to run this on 10 companies, not one)
- Reports that require deep internal cooperation from portfolio company management
- Deliverables that feel like they're designed for a single company, not an investor
- Any hint of the provider sharing portfolio company data between investments

### What gives them the feeling an offer is serious
- Portfolio pricing or volume logic (even if not discounted — just the acknowledgment that they're a repeat buyer)
- Standardized output format that enables cross-portfolio comparison
- Speed — they need to show progress at quarterly portfolio reviews
- Confidentiality signals (NDA readiness, data handling disclosures)

### What they can transmit to a committee
- "External scan identified €X-Y estimated exposure across 5 portfolio companies" — portfolio-level aggregate
- Individual company scorecards ranked by improvement opportunity
- Benchmark data showing where each company sits vs. industry medians

### What creates internal political friction
- If the report makes a portfolio company CEO look bad, the operating partner becomes the villain
- If findings contradict what management reported during fundraising, it creates trust problems
- If the report suggests problems that should have been caught during due diligence, the deal team is embarrassed

### Decision architecture
PE Operating Partners are the highest-leverage buyer. **They buy once and can generate 5-15 repeat purchases across their portfolio.** The key insight: they don't buy cost reduction — they buy "basis points of EBITDA improvement identified in 48 hours." The €490 price point is laughably small against their fund economics. The barrier isn't cost, it's credibility — will this report embarrass them if they share it with a portfolio company CEO? The deliverable must be rigorous enough to survive scrutiny from someone who will be hostile to its findings.

## Cross-Role Tension Map

### The Blame Circuit
Every Ghost Tax report inherently creates an accountability question: "Who should have caught this?" This blame circuit flows differently depending on who buys:
- **CFO buys** → CIO blamed for stack sprawl → CIO challenges methodology
- **CEO buys** → CFO blamed for not knowing → CFO questions data quality
- **CIO buys** → Procurement blamed for not negotiating → Procurement demands contract-level data
- **Procurement buys** → IT blamed for shadow spend → IT says procurement processes are too slow
- **PE buys** → Management team blamed for not optimizing → Management questions outsider's understanding

**Implication for Ghost Tax**: The deliverable must include language that distributes responsibility ("industry-wide pattern" not "your failure") and frames findings as forward-looking opportunity ("estimated recapture potential") not backward-looking blame ("waste you allowed").

### The "Good Enough" Trap
Every buyer has a threshold below which the problem doesn't merit action. For Ghost Tax, the danger zone is findings between 2-5% of IT spend — significant enough to notice, too small to justify a project. The report must either:
1. Aggregate findings to cross the action threshold (individual items are small, combined they're material)
2. Frame findings as leading indicators of larger problems ("if you're overpaying 8% on these 3 tools, statistical likelihood suggests similar patterns across your full portfolio")

### The Committee Transmission Problem
Only the buyer reads the full report. Everyone else sees a summary, a forwarded email, or a verbal mention. Ghost Tax must be designed to survive lossy transmission:
- **Page 1**: Must work as a standalone document (executive summary with 3 findings)
- **Each finding**: Must be expressible in one sentence ("We estimate you're paying 2.1x market rate for your collaboration stack")
- **The methodology section**: Must answer "how do they know this?" in one paragraph — because that's the first question any skeptic asks

### Universal Psychological Drivers

**Fear of underestimating diffuse costs**: Every role knows that small, distributed costs aggregate into material numbers. But nobody wants to be the person who spent 6 months counting pennies. Ghost Tax's value proposition is: "we counted the pennies in 48 hours so you don't have to."

**Fear of opening an endless internal project**: The #1 reason cost optimization doesn't happen isn't that people don't know there's waste — it's that fixing it feels like a 12-month cross-functional nightmare. Ghost Tax must explicitly promise a bounded engagement: "you get a report, not a project."

**Fatigue with tools that promise much and prove little**: Every buyer has been burned by a vendor who promised "full visibility" and delivered a dashboard nobody checks. Ghost Tax's advantage is that it's a one-time deliverable, not a subscription — there's no adoption risk, no implementation, no change management.

**Need to quickly flag a topic without mobilizing 12 teams**: Sometimes the buyer just needs to put "IT cost exposure" on the agenda for the next quarterly review. Ghost Tax is the fastest path from "vague concern" to "specific, citable data point."

**Need to make a problem exist without seeming alarmist**: Saying "I think we're wasting money on IT" sounds like panic. Saying "I commissioned an independent assessment and the findings suggest we should review 3 specific areas" sounds like diligent management.

**Need to prove management discipline without launching a full regulatory audit**: In regulated industries (finance, healthcare), there's pressure to demonstrate cost governance. Ghost Tax provides evidence of management attention without the overhead of a formal audit process. The €490 receipt itself is a governance artifact.
